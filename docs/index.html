Feel the music you already have

Haptiq is a free Android music player that plays the kick drum and the bassline through your phone's vibration motor. It listens while a song plays, looks a beat ahead, and taps the kick and hums the bass against your palm — live, in time, on your own files.

What it is

A music player that plays your skin too.

Most apps treat vibration as a notification tool. Haptiq treats it as an instrument, split into the two things a body actually feels at a concert.

Engine 1

The kick hits.

A kick drum is a sudden jump in low-end energy that falls right back down. Haptiq fires one sharp thump on the jump — then stays quiet until the energy actually falls before it can fire again. That fall is what separates a drum hit from a long bass note — and it's why most risers and sound-effect swells never get past it.

Adaptive

It tunes itself

Every song is mixed differently and every motor is different. The engine tracks how loud each song's own bass gets and moves its trigger points to match — a quiet ballad and a heavy drop both work without touching a slider.

Prefer full control? Switch adaptive off and every threshold is yours.

Engine 2

The bass hums.

The bassline becomes one continuous rumble that rises and falls with the music — not an on-off buzz. Louder bass, harder push; when it fades, the motor fades with it, like a speaker cone following the wave.

Sidechain

Punch stays clean

When a kick fires, the rumble ducks out of the way for a fraction of a second so the thump stays distinct.

808-aware

One sound, two jobs

If the bass swells right back after a kick, it's one 808 doing both jobs — the rumble returns early, softer, like the real tail.

Character

Drum or 808?

A real kick carries a high "click" from the beater. Haptiq hears it and adds a snap on top; a clickless 808 gets a pure deep thud.

Any motor.

Haptiq checks once what your motor can do — composed pulses, timed amplitude, or plain on-off — and speaks to it in its own language.

AOT lookahead

A beat ahead

The scheduler pre-fires each kick by your motor's measured spin-up time, so the motor is already moving when the audio hits — live-only detection would land a beat late.

Character

SNAP → HEAVY

Every kick is a decaying strike-train you can shape: a 35ms SNAP tick or a 240ms HEAVY thump whose re-strikes keep the motor moving through surface damping.

Teach it

See the beat, place the kick

When a song fools auto-detection, the visual editor shows the waveform, every transient, and the beat grid — tap a peak and the kick snaps to the nearest real hit.

Under the hood

Real parts, no magic.

Analysis

20 reads a second

The audio spectrum is read about twenty times per second — enough to catch every beat without draining the battery.

Detection

Behavior, not bands

Kicks are found by how energy moves — sharp rise, real fall — not just by which frequencies are loud.

Rendering

Native vibration

Thud and click primitives on modern motors, amplitude waveforms on older ones, duty-cycling on basic motors.

Stack

Kotlin + Compose

Jetpack Compose UI, Media3/ExoPlayer playback, Room for your library. Standard Android, no exotic dependencies.

Calibration

Knows your phone

A guided wizard measures your motor once and stores a strength profile for your exact device.

Privacy

Zero network

No account, no ads, no analytics. The audio-capture permission Android requires is used only to read Haptiq's own playback — never the microphone around you.

Straight answers

What Haptiq is not.

Not a subwoofer

A subwoofer moves air; a phone motor moves millimeters. Haptiq doesn't pretend otherwise — it uses sharp timing, real silence between hits, and rumble that follows the music to make your hands believe what your chest would feel.

Not the same on every phone

Motors range from precise linear actuators to basic spinning weights. Haptiq detects what yours can do and adapts. It's tuned daily on a budget phone, so a better motor only makes it feel better.

Not watching you

No account, no ads, no analytics, no network use. Your music, your phone, full stop.

Not finished

Haptiq is in active development, versioned one feel-test at a time. The commit list below is live from the repository — what you see is exactly where the work is.

The journey

From one buzz to an engine.

Haptiq started in July 2026 with a simple question: phones have had vibration motors forever — why does music never use them well? The first version was a player with a single crude pulse. Everything since has been built by playing real songs on a real phone and asking: does this feel right?

v1.01 – v1.05

The foundation: local player, library scanning, the first kick/bass engine, the Paper Tactile design, and the motor calibration wizard.

v1.06 – v1.08

Per-song artwork, gesture fixes, an accessibility pass, and the labelled Up Next queue.

v1.09

Native playlist engine — the whole queue lives inside the player, so auto-advance and notification skips just work.

v1.10

The haptic feel overhaul: bass became a continuous envelope-following rumble instead of an on-off buzz, and the kick got its punch back.

v1.11

Separate kick and bass intensity controls, a re-arm gate that stops sound-effect swells from faking beats, 808 handling, and per-motor capability detection.

v1.12

Haptic latency fixes, AOT kick lookahead with a beat grid, a kick-onset seek-bar overlay, and a motion + contrast audit with reduced-motion support.

v1.13

The kick-character axis — SNAP, PUNCH, THUMP, HEAVY — rendered as decaying strike-trains, presets renamed to match, and a smarter adaptive engine with a surface feedback loop.

v1.14

The beat-grid drift fix (tempo detection that hugs the music instead of quantizing), a motor-calibrated pre-fire lead, and teach kicks — the first version of the waveform editor.

v1.15 — current

The editor became a real tool: pan, pinch-zoom, drag-to-scrub audition and an “At playhead” button — plus shareable kick maps and a mini player that opens with a tap.
