Feel the music you already have.

Haptiq is a free Android music player that turns your phone's vibration motor into a tiny subwoofer. It listens to the song as it plays and taps the kick drum and hums the bassline against your palm — live, in time, on your own files.

What it does

Two engines, one motor.

Most music apps treat vibration as a notification tool. Haptiq treats it as an instrument, split into the two things a body actually feels at a concert.

The kick hits

When the song's kick drum lands, the motor fires one sharp thump — the same decisive snap you feel tapping a button, timed to the beat. After each hit the engine waits for the sound to actually drop before it can fire again, so long risers and sound-effect swells can't make it stutter.

The bass hums

The bassline becomes one continuous rumble that rises and falls with the music — not an on-off buzz. Louder bass, harder push. When the bass fades, the motor fades with it, like a speaker cone following the wave.

It tunes itself

Every song is mixed differently and every phone motor is different. The engine tracks how loud each song's own bass gets and moves its trigger points to match, so a quiet ballad and a heavy drop both work without touching a slider.

The engine

How a song becomes touch.

01

Read the spectrum

While a song plays, Haptiq reads its frequency spectrum about twenty times a second — enough to catch every beat without draining the battery.

02

Spot the kick by its behavior

A kick drum is a sudden jump in low-end energy that falls right back down. The engine fires on the jump, then stays quiet until the energy actually falls — that fall is what separates a drum hit from a long bass note, and it's why sustained sounds can't fake a beat.

03

Follow the bass like a wave

The bassline is tracked with a smoothed envelope: a running measure of how loud the bass is right now compared to the song's own recent peak. That number drives the rumble strength directly.

04

Keep the punch clean

When a kick fires, the bass rumble ducks out of the way for a fraction of a second so the thump stays distinct. If the bass swells right back, the engine recognizes one 808 doing both jobs and lets the rumble return early, softer.

05

Speak the motor's language

Phones differ. On newer motors Haptiq plays composed thud-and-click pulses; on older ones, timed amplitude; on basic motors that only switch on and off, it fakes loudness with rapid duty-cycling. The app checks what your motor can do once, then always uses the best option it has.

Straight answers

What Haptiq is not.

Not a subwoofer

A subwoofer moves air; a phone motor moves millimeters. Haptiq doesn't pretend otherwise — it uses sharp timing, real silence between hits, and rumble that follows the music to make your hands believe what your chest would feel.

Not the same on every phone

Motors range from precise linear actuators to basic spinning weights. Haptiq detects what yours can do and adapts. It's built and tuned daily on a budget phone, so a better motor only makes it feel better.

Not watching you

No account, no ads, no analytics, no network use. Android requires the audio-capture permission for the visualizer API — Haptiq uses it only to read its own playback, never the microphone around you.

Not finished

Haptiq is in active development, versioned one feel-test at a time. The commit list below is live from the repository — what you see is exactly where the work is.

The journey

From one buzz to an engine.

Haptiq started in July 2026 with a simple question: phones have had vibration motors forever — why does music never use them well? The first version was a player with a single crude pulse. Everything since has been built by playing real songs on a real phone and asking, does this feel right?

v1.01 – v1.05

The foundation: local player, library scanning, the first kick/bass engine, the Paper Tactile design, and the motor calibration wizard.

v1.06 – v1.08

Per-song artwork, gesture fixes, an accessibility pass, and the labelled Up Next queue.

v1.09

Native playlist engine — the whole queue lives inside the player, so auto-advance and notification skips just work.

v1.10

The haptic feel overhaul: bass became a continuous envelope-following rumble instead of an on-off buzz, and the kick got its punch back.

v1.11 — current

Separate kick and bass intensity controls, a re-arm gate that stops sound-effect swells from faking beats, 808 handling, and per-motor capability detection.
